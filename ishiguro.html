How Narrative Voice Constructs Irony in The Remains of the Day

In Neil Schaeffer’s essay on irony, he states that “The satirist need not praise evil, the preacher’s sin, and the doctor’s disease. But the ironicist must become the evil he condemns, impersonate the fool he disdains” (Schaeffer 182). Unlike satire, which criticizes from a safe distance, irony, specifically in fiction, often inhabits the voice, the mind of the very person whose flaws or limitations are being exposed. The irony doesn’t necessarily arise from distant and direct critique, but rather from the discrepancy between the narrator’s self-image and the reader’s understanding of the narrative. Ishiguro takes this principle and applies it to the entire narrative of The Remains of the Day through the voice of Stevens, whose unswerving dedication to his “dignity” masks his moral failure. Rather than overtly condemning Stevens, Ishiguro carefully allows the reader to witness his self-justifications and hesitations. By impersonating the fool he disdains, Ishiguro becomes the “ironicist” Schaeffer describes. In The Remains of the Day, the use of unreliable narration is not just a stylistic device but rather functions as an ironic structure. The form of the novel — an unreliable retrospective memoir, delayed revelation, and narrative gaps — thus becomes the site of its deepest irony, as Stevens himself unwittingly dramatizes the cost of his misplaced loyalties.

Unreliable narration as structural irony

In The Remains of the Day, Ishiguro constructs an ironic narrative structure through the lens of Stevens, whose role as an unreliable narrator is predominant in both the form and the outcome of the novel. An unreliable narrator is when a narrator’s credibility is compromised, whether it is through bias, self-deception, or intentional distortion of the content. In Stevens’s case, his unreliability does not stem from evil or dishonesty, but rather from a deeply internalized commitment to dignity and duty, which blinds him to his moral failings. A dedication to formal presence leaves him emotionally repressed. Such unreliability is not simply a character trait but serves as the foundation for the novel’s structural irony — a sustained gap between what Stevens believes and what the reader comes to understand. A contradiction between the narrator’s perspective and the reality that the reader gradually perceives. As Linda Hutcheon proposes in her essay “The Complex Functions of Irony,” “What would happen if we thought of irony in terms of a dynamic relationship, a communicative process?” (Hutcheon 220). Such a process requires the interplay between “the said and the unsaid” which then must “play off against each other… in order for such a process even to be recognized as ironic” (Hutcheon 220). Comparatively, Ishiguro employs this process structurally in the novel, requiring the reader to actively interpret “the said and the unsaid”: what Stevens says and what the text reveals. In Stevens’s case, the discrepancy between his voice and the reader’s understanding of events becomes a sustained irony that is embedded in the retrospective memoir form of the novel.

Ishiguro deliberately constructs Stevens’s narrative as a series of reflections that seems linear, a five-day journey through the English countryside that is constantly interrupted by flashbacks and delayed revelations. These fragmented memories slowly expose Stevens’s moral failures, specifically his misplaced loyalty to Lord Darlington, a man that is revealed to have supported fascist ideologies. Early in the novel, Stevens states “Lord Darlington was a gentleman of great moral stature — a true old-fashioned English gentleman” (Ishiguro 114). Through layering the past and the present, the reader comes to learn the naiveness of Stevens’s unwavering belief. Ishiguro withholds critical facts, such as Lord Darlington’s political stance or Stevens’s complicity in firing two Jewish maids, until later in the narrative. Rather than openly acknowledging these failures after some delayed revelations, Stevens frames them in the language of professionalism and dedication to duty: “It was a difficult situation, but I believed I was doing what was required of me” (Ishiguro 155). Stevens has constructed his identity around an empty ideal, and the form of the novel is able to reflect the psychological makeup of self-deception. Rather than offering resolution, the narrative arc bends toward entrenchment. The symbolic journey, the road trip, therefore fails to produce transformation.

As Rebecca Walkowitz argues in her essay “Ishiguro’s Treason,” Stevens’s self-deception and diction is carefully constructed to mask feelings and responsibilities, specifically the choice of “One” over “I”: “Stevens’s language seems at once natural — what a butler sounds like — and yet tactical. ‘One’ negates the claim to personal feeling Stevens’s statement would otherwise offer… If a distinctive artlessness is the hallmark of English manners, it is the accomplishment of this effect, everywhere boasted by Stevens, that nevertheless gives away the self-delusion beneath” (Walkowitz 126). Here, Walkowitz identifies how Stevens’s Englishness becomes a shield that enables his self-deception. Even at the end of the novel, rather than confronting the emotional costs of his loyalty and restraint, Stevens resolves merely to learn to “banter.” The novel closes not with self-awareness, but with Stevens’s recommitment to a worldview that perpetuates his delusions.

Moreover, this form of irony, rooted in narrative voice and structure, aligns with Neil Schaeffer’s observation that “the ironicist must become the evil he condemns, impersonate the fool he disdains” (Schaeffer 182). Ishiguro does not condemn Stevens from a distance; instead he writes from within Stevens’s limited perspective, allowing the character’s delusions and evasions to slowly expose himself. By doing so, it invites the reader to perform the task of interpretation, in the process discovering Stevens’s blind spots not through contradiction, but rather through accumulation. The reader is placed in a morally active role, being a participant in deciphering the truth through narrative gaps and tonal tensions.

Situational irony within The Remains of the Day

While The Remains of the Day is built upon structural irony, sustained through Stevens’s retrospective narration and self-deception, it is also enhanced by moments of situational irony that reinforce and deepen the novel’s tragic underlayers. Situational irony arises when an event or outcome of said event contradicts the expectations of the character or reader. For instance, one of the most ironic moments comes during his meeting with Miss Kenton, whom he has not seen in twenty years and whose memory has animated much of his journey. Stevens wanted a possibility to reconnect, suggesting: “it is, of course, possible that her new life has not turned out quite as she had wished,” and that she may even be “ready to consider returning” (Ishiguro 176). Such anticipation, embedded within the narrative structure as intermissions during his trip, functions to build an expectation that is abruptly torn down. When Miss Kenton tells him: “I’ve grown to be very fond of my husband, I’ve grown to depend on him” (Ishiguro 239), her firm delivery deals an emotional blow precisely because it shatters the constructed tension that the narrative was able to build through delayed revelation and reflective buildup.

These moments of irony are heightened by Stevens’s controlled, formal language, which often works to obscure rather than express feeling. Ishiguro’s use of precise and passive phrasing exposes the gaps between Stevens’s emotional reality and what is being presented on the surface narratively. For instance, after recounting the dismissal of the Jewish maids at Lord Darlington’s request, Stevens reflects: “I did what I believed to be my duty. I cannot see that I could have done otherwise” (Ishiguro 148). The repetition of duty, the denial of self-agency in “I cannot see,” and the absence of emotional vocabulary all contribute to the tone of repression. However, to the readers, such justification is deeply ironic: Stevens’s word choices and phrasing reveal what he cannot admit, that he has betrayed his own moral instinct in favor of loyalty. As Wall writes in her essay, “We note that the feelings [Stevens] report conflict with his behavior… His commentary is at odds with the evidence presented in the scenes he narrates” (Wall 25). It highlights how Stevens’s formal language contradicts his emotional reality. The novel’s structural form, which withholds this event until midway through the novel, allows the reader to question earlier moments of Stevens’s narrative, deepening the sense of irony over time. Like how Eatough argues in his essay, “The Remains of the Day provides a retrospective, revisionist account of the interwar years, with Stevens gradually coming to understand that his idealization of ‘professional’ service to ‘distinguished households’ is uncomfortably similar to the exclusionary, hierarchical world envisioned by Nazism.” Stevens’s narration, with its tone of correctness, masks a deeper repression and ideological complicity. This disjuncture between form and content is ultimately what makes it all ironic.

The use of situational irony also appears in Ishiguro’s Klara and the Sun, where the narrator’s limited understanding creates moments of emotional tension that extend beyond the narrator’s awareness. In this case, similar to Stevens, Klara’s voice is marked by precision, formality, and a deep commitment to her duty as an Artificial Friend and the conviction that the Sun has certain healing powers that can save Josie. The tragic irony emerges when Klara offers a ritualistic act, begging the Sun to heal Josie by making a promise to destroy the Cooting Machine: “I ask you, Sun, to be kind. I ask you kindly to make Josie better. I’ll do all I can to help you. I’ll do whatever’s necessary” (Klara and the Sun 204). To her, this is a meaningful act, yet the reader perceives it as heartbreakingly naive. It reveals a disconnect between her intentions and reality. As in The Remains of the Day, Ishiguro constructs this irony not by mocking the narrator, but by allowing the reader to understand more than the narrator can. The appeal to pathos in both novels lies in the careful layering of the narrators’ formal, orderly voices attempting to make sense of a world that evades them, an outsider, exposing the limits of loyalty and belief through calm but devastating irony.

Framing the journey, retrospection as a vehicle for self-deception

The narrative arc of The Remains of the Day is framed around a seemingly chronological forward road trip, a structure that is conventionally associated with personal transformation, emotional openness, or the possibility of catharsis. However, Ishiguro subverts this expectation, using episodic, fragmented narration to reveal Stevens’s journey serves not to clarify the past but rather obscures it. In the beginning, Stevens claims his goal was to enjoy a short holiday and to see the countryside; however, his true motivation is revealed gradually, that is to see Miss Kenton, with it all being filtered through vague rationalization and professional justifications. The realized structure of the novel mirrors the actual working of his mind: chronological forward movement that is disrupted by uninvited memories and narrative detours. What seems, at first, to be a memoir of dignified reflection becomes instead a portrait of repression and narrative control. Stevens’s journey becomes circular rather than progressive, an elegant facade that conceals the truth it claims to pursue. This contradiction between external movement and internal stasis emphasizes the novel’s core irony: Stevens is going somewhere physically, but emotionally, he cannot move forward.

As John Rothfork notes in his essay “Zen Comedy in Postcolonial Literature: Kazuo Ishiguro’s The Remains of the Day,” rather than culminating in a moral revelation, Stevens’s road trip ends with an almost theatrical reaffirmation of his butler persona. Stevens’s hope “to make the best of what remains of my day” is, as Rothfork writes, “Having no assigned part to play, one has no fear of giving a bad performance. In retiring from the world, as do Buddhist monks, there is an invitation to see life as art, as a performance rather than as a Zoroastrian battle” (Rothfork 93). By subverting the expectation of progress, Ishiguro reframes the journey not as self-discovery but as self-containment — an aesthetic performance of endurance over transformation.

Moreover, the circular nature of the road trip reflects a broader thematic concern, which is that memory, when it is filtered through ideology, can fail to produce growth upon reflection. Stevens’s reflections are not governed by a desire for any objective truth, but rather by a compulsion to uphold certain values such as dignity, service, and restraint. As Rothfork suggests, the novel offers a kind of “Zen comedy,” in which failure is neither tragic nor is it redemptive: “Instead of living they are acting. Then one sees this about one’s self. And suddenly the role of samurai or butler or even monk is transformed from a matter of humorless and grim discipline into a performance, a dance. The axis shifts from counting the minute details of duty to appreciating an esthetic performance. Life is not confined to a number of Confucian games” (98). It is not a spiritual journey or moral reckoning, but an extension of the role he has always played. The novel thus subverts the conventional expectations of the travel narrative, where movement typically implies insight or transformation, and instead uses the journey form to reveal the depth of Stevens’s repression. The refusal of narrative closure, by doing so, turns the journey into a vehicle not of self-discovery, but of ironic self-containment.

In addition to reframing the road trip genre, Ishiguro also subverts the conventions of the traditional British estate novel, a genre that is historically rooted in stability, hierarchy, and nostalgia. In The Remains of the Day, Darlington Hall is not a site of moral authority, but rather of political naivety and repression. Stevens idealizes the estate as a place where “great men” shape history and where he finds meaning through servitude: “Stevens, this is a genuine grand old English house, isn’t it? That’s what I paid for. And you’re a genuine old-fashioned English butler, not just some waiter pretending to be one. You’re the real thing, aren’t you?” (Ishiguro 124). As Eatough writes, Ishiguro’s “novel’s real target is the nostalgic representations of country estates common in the 1980s — as perhaps best emblematized by the popular BBC adaptations of Brideshead Revisited, Mansfield Park, and other famous estate novels — which Ishiguro presents as idealized historical fantasies insufficiently attuned to the inequalities on which such estates were built” (Eatough 44).

Darlington Hall is no longer the inheritance of a noble legacy, but rather a shell repurposed as a tourist attraction. Stevens, too, is no longer in service of cultural memory but is revealed as a deluded participant. The house is physically intact but morally corrupt, and its transformation underscores the dissociation of the aristocratic world that Stevens so dearly holds onto. This way, Ishiguro not only critiques the estate novel’s romanticism, but uses its form ironically, inviting the readers to mourn not only a lost nobility but also the narrator’s inability to see past the ideological system that has failed him. It is almost anti-nostalgia in a way that is disguised as dignity.

Connection between The Remains of the Day, Klara and the Sun, and Never Let Me Go

A similar form of structural irony appears in Klara and the Sun, where Ishiguro once again crafts a narrative around a first-person voice that is dedicated to their duties, sincere and emotionally restrained, someone who is yet to fully grasp the implications of the world they inhabit. Klara, an Artificial Friend designed to care for children, narrates her observations with unwavering faith in the Sun’s healing powers, believing that her sacrifice, by destroying the Cooting Machine, will help cure Josie’s disease: “I know how much the Sun dislikes Pollution. How much it saddens and angers you. Well, I’ve seen and identified the machine that creates it. Supposing I were able somehow to find this machine and destroy it… in return, giving your special help to Josie?” (Klara and the Sun 164). While Klara perceives her actions as meaningful and effective, the reader recognizes the tragic irony under the surface. Josie’s recovery has nothing to do with Klara’s efforts, and her belief system simply reflects a predetermined and programmed logic rather than spiritual understanding. This structural irony, built into Klara’s innocent voice, echoes Stevens’s formal correctness and emotional restraint. Both narrators perform roles defined by service, and both are constructed to function within systems that ultimately use and discard them. In Ishiguro’s conversation with Ōe Kenzaburo, he describes his narrators as “homeless” voices, unmoored from national identity (Ishiguro and Oe 115). Ishiguro’s method aligns with Ōe’s praise for his “double-structured” style (Ishiguro and Oe 114).

Such structural irony is also apparent in Never Let Me Go, where Kathy narrates her life at Hailsham and her involvement with the donation program, while never fully articulating the tragic horror of the institution that she is part of. Similarly to Stevens and Klara, Kathy’s narration is shaped by what she cannot say; her conditioning has suppressed her language for resistance. Ishiguro again uses structural irony avoiding any direct critique, something that a traditional science fiction would usually tackle. All three narrators — Stevens, Klara, and Kathy — are outsiders whose loyalty to flawed systems isolates them from any self-awareness. In each case, irony is not just an external structure but also an internal contradiction.

Kazuo Ishiguro’s use of narrative voice in The Remains of the Day alters a restrained and emotionally distant memoir and develops it into a powerful tool for structural irony that in turn functions not only as external commentary but also as an examination of one’s internal contradictions. Through Stevens’s unreliable narration, delayed revelations, narrative gaps, and emotionally repressed tones, the novel not only critiques personal failures, but the idealized outlook of duty, and social and economic class disparities. By adopting the forms of different genres, in this case the estate novel and the road trip, Ishiguro embeds irony into the very structure of the novel, inviting the readers to uncover what the narrator ultimately cannot. A similar dynamic can be seen in Ishiguro’s other novel, Klara and the Sun, where the limitations of an innocent narrator demonstrate the emotional costs of belief and devotion. Ishiguro reenacts Schaeffer’s definition of the ironicist — that is, to not condemn from above but rather impersonate the fool, allowing irony to emerge from within.
