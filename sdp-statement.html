Senior Degree Project Statement — May 2026

Unstable Systems

Everyday sonic environments function as chaotic systems.

Alongside painting and sculptural objects, I have been primarily focused on creating sound pieces. Sonic experience thrives when visual authority becomes unstable and sensory hierarchies collapse. When visual dominance weakens, sound reorganises spatial perception, turning the artwork from an object into an environment.

I often wonder what it means for something to be transformative or imaginative — not as spectacle, but as a way of recalibrating how one senses. I want to examine how perception is conditioned by systems operating beneath conscious awareness. Are things ever neutral? It isn't only visual manipulation but the other senses too. A certain smell can evoke a memory. A certain sound can be so programmed into us that we rarely think about it.

Colour is never neutral

Like in painting, things are formed through pushing and pulling, foreground and background. There is something strange and fascinating about the unknown — what is behind the paint.

Colour is never neutral; it is trained and associative. A certain green doesn't just sit there. It reflects hospital, night vision, toxic waste, freshness. Those associations aren't inherent to the pigment. They are pushed through repetition, culture and exposure.

How many hidden systems are there that we rarely think about? Low frequencies running constantly. Nature follows a certain set of systems, and so the human experience has its constraints too.

Two systems

In my work I navigate two specific systems: the system of painting and the system of sound synthesis.

Painting has inherent structures that were predetermined — foreground and background relationships, light, composition and framing, gestural versus controlled. Painting itself can be treated as a system that conditions perception. It compresses space and time into a static frame, stabilising perception.

Sound, by contrast, disperses perception into time and space. Modular synthesis contains rules bound to our understanding of physics, but there is no fixed signal path, which means the system can be constantly reorganised. It is unstable, and that instability is where I find the most joy in making work.

Synthesis consists of oscillators, filters, sequencers and envelope generators, connected by the user through patch cables. Nothing is predetermined; every connection is reconfigurable. Working this way allows for discovery and unpredictability, and demonstrates the tension between an existing structure and its tear-down.

Sound as material

Situating these sonic negotiations within established musical traditions highlights their deviation from linear, time-based composition. Rather than following a predetermined structure, sound is treated as material — layered, interrupted, reconfigured.

This is what draws me to it as the most painterly form of sound: a process of continual adjustment and manipulation, where relationships are built and torn down through proximity, density and interference rather than sequence.

Graphic scores

I have been looking at a lot of graphic transcription, and at how sound compositions — stable systems — are organised visually. 40 Cryptograms by Mark Applebaum draws a great deal of my attention. The composer has described the idea of performing it as a musical work as somewhat of a joke to me at the time.

I often think about

Unstable systems.
The breakdown of authority.
Reconfigurable structures.

40 Cryptograms does exactly that. The score appears systematic, yet it produces no fixed result. Every performance interprets its symbols and language differently, inventing different sonic logics, producing a new system born out of instability. Treating the graphic score as a general guideline highlights the very tension that drives my work: the tension between structured systems and interpretive collapse.

Kandinsky proposed fixed correspondences between colour and sound. My work approaches both as contingent systems, where associations are learned, unstable, and shaped by technological and cultural filters.

Not resolution — tension

My work does not seek to resolve these systems, but to keep them in a state of tension, where perception is constantly negotiated rather than passively received. By destabilising visual authority and foregrounding sound as a spatial and temporal force, I aim to expose how deeply conditioned our sensory experiences are, and how easily they can be reorganised.

What emerges is not a new fixed system, but an awareness of their constant construction and collapse. Transformation is no longer a spectacle or an illusion.
